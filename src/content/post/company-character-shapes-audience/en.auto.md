Over the years, I've increasingly come to believe in one idea: **a company's core identity determines the kind of users it ultimately attracts.**

Many people discussing cars and phones like to attribute brand preference to product strength, specifications, price, or even marketing prowess. But for me, what truly influences purchasing decisions has never been a specific generation of products, but rather the product philosophy a company has cultivated over decades, and the values it represents.

I've always believed that in a consumerist society, every consumption choice is a selection of the part of the world one desires.

Buying a car or a phone isn't just acquiring a tool; it's also deciding which companies receive more resources, which product philosophies continue to evolve, and which R&D directions deserve market reward. The money I spend isn't just exchanged for a commodity; it's a vote for a world I endorse.

Precisely for this reason, I never evaluate a single generation of products in isolation. I'm more concerned with why a company makes such products, how it thinks about them, and what kind of company it aspires to be.

## What's Truly Hard for a Company to Change is Its Core Identity

Many believe that a company, by continuously progressing, can ultimately become anything it wants to be. But I don't think so. I believe what's hardest for a company to change is never a specific technology, but rather its long-accumulated organizational capabilities and product philosophy.

A company's experiences over decades determine how it defines products, allocates R&D resources, evaluates success, and confronts competition. These collectively form the company's core identity.

Business performance can fluctuate, products can succeed or fail, CEOs can change, but what truly determines a company's long-term character often transcends many eras.

Apple and Microsoft are excellent examples.

Many would say that Apple in the nineties was very chaotic, and that's certainly true. But in my view, that was more a matter of operational issues than product philosophy. From Macintosh to Newton, and then to iMac and iPhone, Apple has consistently adhered to the same way of thinking for decades: product definition first, experience first, hardware-software integration, and industrial design serving the overall experience. This underlying logic has barely changed.

Microsoft, on the other hand, is completely different. Decades of accumulation have led Microsoft to develop a different skill tree: platforms, developer ecosystems, enterprise software, and global cloud infrastructure. This is why I've always believed that Microsoft couldn't make an iPhone, and Apple likewise couldn't make Azure. It's not because one company is more capable than the other, but because they've developed in different directions since their inception, ultimately forming distinct organizational capabilities.

## The Same Applies to Car Brands

Regarding car manufacturers, I've long identified with Nio, not because every generation of its products is leading, nor because of specific selling points like service or battery swapping, but because I endorse the development path it represents.

In my view, Nio has consistently focused on building systemic capabilities. From vehicle platforms, electrical and electronic architecture, and software systems, to battery swap networks, energy replenishment systems, and then to user service and brand expression, it aims to construct a complete set of capabilities, rather than just a single point of advantage. This kind of capability building often involves huge investment and slow returns, sometimes even failing to directly translate into sales for a considerable period, yet it can form truly long-term competitive barriers. I particularly admire its restraint and consistency in design, and its consistent international product aesthetic, rather than deliberately catering to market sentiment.

In contrast, Li Auto and Xiaomi represent a different corporate philosophy. They are more adept at adapting to trends, quickly integrating resources with extremely high execution efficiency after market demand has been validated, and refining existing mature solutions to better meet mass market needs. This is a typical efficiency-driven competition, pursuing faster product iteration, more accurate market judgment, and higher business efficiency. From a business perspective, this is undoubtedly a very successful development model and a key reason for their rapid rise.

But if I had to choose, I would still prefer the former.

I consistently admire companies willing to spend a decade building systems, establishing standards, and cultivating long-term capabilities, rather than merely optimizing efficiency within existing tracks. I prefer systemic innovation from **0 to 1** over efficiency-driven competition from **1 to N**. The former creates a new capability, while the latter optimizes an existing one; the former defines the future, the latter adapts to the present. Both are worthy of respect, but my values consistently lean towards the former.

Therefore, even if Li Auto's chassis becomes industry-leading in the future, or Xiaomi produces the electric car with the strongest overall qualities, I might still not become their user. Because what truly matters to me has never been a specific product capability, but rather the long-term character demonstrated by the company.

This character is reflected in product language, in brand expression, and even more so in how a company handles competition, faces the market, and communicates with users.

Some companies consistently maintain restraint, while others are always keen on creating buzz; some are willing to accumulate slowly through long-term R&D, while others emphasize quickly responding to market demands; some hope for the product to be the expression itself, while others are more accustomed to letting marketing precede the product.

These things don't suddenly disappear just because one generation of products succeeds.

## Products May Change, Product Definition May Not

This is also why I've always believed that what's truly hard for a company to change isn't technology, but its method of defining products.

Taking the phone industry as an example, I've often said in the past that Xiaomi's product definition consistently revolves around cost and price point. This isn't a criticism, but an observation. When a company is long accustomed to first determining cost and then considering how to allocate specifications, the capability it develops naturally becomes how to optimize paper specifications.

Apple's approach, conversely, tends to first answer "What should this product be?" and then decide what hardware is needed to achieve it.

Consequently, when both companies set out to make a small-sized flagship, the resulting products will be entirely different.

In my view, the greatest value of a small-sized product lies in portability and balance, not in cramming in all flagship specifications. Therefore, when a small-screen phone, in pursuit of stronger specs, continuously increases in weight, expands its camera module, and sacrifices grip experience, I feel it has begun to deviate from its own product definition. This isn't a lack of engineering capability, but a conflict in product philosophy.

## A Company's Core Identity Filters Its Users

Therefore, I increasingly agree with the statement: **a company's core identity determines the kind of people it ultimately attracts.**

Brands and consumers are, in fact, engaged in a two-way selection. The values a company consistently upholds will gradually attract people who identify with those values, and these users, in turn, further strengthen the brand culture.

Of course, this doesn't mean every user shares the same character. Any brand with millions of users will certainly have all kinds of people. But the core users who truly stay long-term often develop similar value orientations, because they identify not just with the product itself, but with the methodology the company adheres to.

This is also why I find it increasingly difficult to change my overall assessment of a company merely because one generation of its products is excellent.

Products can catch up, technology can be pursued, design can be reshaped, but a company's long-established organizational culture, product philosophy, and brand identity are not things that can be changed in a year or two.

Therefore, what I consume has never been just the product itself.

What I purchase is a company's long-held methodology; what I support is a product philosophy I wish to see continue; and what I choose is an industry direction I hope will sustainably develop in the future.

**Because I consistently believe that a company's core identity determines its products, products determine their audience, and every act of consumption is ultimately a choice of the part of the world one desires.**
