Today's tech industry is keen on discussing AI, ecosystems, intelligence, and the Internet of Everything, but rarely touches upon an increasingly scarce resource—attention.

Product managers discuss user growth, activity, and dwell time, as well as interaction efficiency, learning curves, and task completion rates. Yet, few ask another question: How much of a user's attention does a product consume each day?

The attention I speak of isn't simply about reducing ads or notifications, nor is it merely minimalism. It's a more fundamental design philosophy: human attention is finite, and it should be reserved for truly important matters, not wasted on understanding, adapting to, or learning a product.

I later realized that many products I've liked over the years—be it an iPhone, a Tesla, or various software tools—seem unrelated but share a common trait: they rarely make users focus their attention on the product itself.

---

What truly made me realize this was an experience I had recently while running an errand at a government office.

The staff member was verifying my documents, and I needed to quickly find a file on my phone. I had both an iPhone and an Android phone with me. I instinctively picked up the Android one, intending only to exit the current app and return to the home screen. However, my finger slid a little too high while swiping up the navigation bar, and the system immediately entered split-screen mode. The entire interface instantly transformed: the upper half remained on the previous app, while the lower half displayed a full page of app listings, along with new interaction options like split-screen and floating window.

Of course, I knew what this feature was and how to exit it. But what truly made me pause wasn't a lack of operational knowledge. At that moment, my attention was almost entirely focused on the administrative process—I was thinking about whether my documents were in order, what the staff member had just said, and where I needed to go next. The phone was merely a temporary tool to look up information. When it suddenly entered a completely unexpected state, I had to pull my attention away from the task at hand, understand why it had changed, and only then could I continue with my original action.

Afterward, I kept thinking that what truly consumes attention isn't often the feature itself, but rather those unexpected state transitions. An operation with a clear objective, due to a slight gesture deviation, suddenly enters a different interaction logic. The system starts guessing the user's intent, and the user, in turn, has to guess what the system has interpreted. The real loss of attention occurs in this back-and-forth.

This preference actually runs through my judgment of many products.

Take mobile wallets, for example. I've always liked the iPhone's double-press power button design. Many Android systems offer various quick access points: double-pressing the power button, long-pressing the fingerprint sensor, swiping from a screen corner, or accessing it from the control center. This seems to offer more freedom, but payments almost always happen in places like convenience stores, subway turnstiles, or mall checkouts, where actions need to be completed quickly. I don't want to have to recall which opening methods my phone supports. Double-pressing the power button has become muscle memory: pick up the phone, double-press, pay. The entire process requires almost no thought, and attention remains focused on the act of payment itself, rather than on how many ways the product offers to achieve it.

Many people have long hoped for the iPhone to include Touch ID, offering an additional biometric method alongside Face ID. I've never been particularly interested in this; Face ID has established a very natural interaction: pick up the phone, glance, done. For certain specific scenarios, an extra authentication method can certainly solve problems. However, in the vast majority of daily use cases, each added interaction means another branch of interaction that users need to think about and choose from. Product design often focuses on adding features and constantly increasing new options, but ensuring users always know what will happen next and controlling the number of possibilities is equally important in design.

---

This feeling later extended to home appliances.

I've always liked traditional Japanese electric kettles. A single physical button: press it blindly to instantly start dispensing water, release to stop. The entire process requires no learning. Many smart electric kettles now use capacitive buttons, network connectivity, app control, and various keep-warm modes. These features aren't without value, but I just want a cup of hot water. I don't want to have to confirm if a button has registered each time I use it, or open my phone for a mundane action. For a product used daily, if each use requires even a tiny bit more attention, these small consumptions accumulate and eventually become a burden on the experience.

This sentiment also explains why I've always been able to understand a segment of Tesla users.

People often discuss online why Tesla continues to sell so well. Many analyses revolve around brand, loyalty, autonomous driving, or performance, with some even suggesting Tesla users haven't experienced better products. However, if someone genuinely wants an electric car that minimizes attention dissipation, there aren't many options on the market. Many brands constantly add features, entry points, and modes, hoping to demonstrate product strength through abundance. Yet, another segment of users is truly willing to pay for a system that remains consistent, without needing to learn a new entry point today or adapt to a new logic tomorrow. For them, the more easily the product recedes into the background, the more attention can be dedicated to driving itself.

NIO also gives me a similar feeling, though this experience isn't carried through to the end.

I've always felt that NIO's industrial design and in-car system design are quite restrained, and the brand generally conveys a unified impression, attracting many users like me who value interaction quality. However, upon opening the app, one encounters a completely different logic. Activities, a marketplace, a community, crowdfunding, and various operational content constantly reach users through pushes. Each message, viewed individually, has a reason for existing, but collectively, they occupy the same resource—the user's attention. An app that primarily serves as a car key begins to constantly remind you to participate in events, browse the marketplace, or join discussions. This sense of disconnect is essentially the same issue as the accidental split-screen activation mentioned earlier; both force users to shift their attention back to the product rather than their own goals.

---

Writing this, I realize that what I've been pursuing isn't minimalism. Minimalism is merely a final form; what truly matters to me is whether a product respects the user's attention. I've found a more precise term for this experience: certainty.

Certainty is not a design style, but a method for managing attention. One interaction corresponds to one clear outcome, one action to one stable feedback, and one entry point to one fixed responsibility. When these relationships are sufficiently stable, the user's brain gradually converts them into muscle memory, and the product slowly recedes from the conscious to the subconscious level. Users don't need to repeatedly understand or adapt to it, nor will a routine operation lead them into a completely unfamiliar state. Their limited attention is naturally preserved for what truly matters.

Today's tech industry has begun to value computing resources, storage resources, and energy management, yet rarely treats attention as a design object that requires careful management. Many products still view user attention as a commercial resource, hoping to gain more dwell time and higher activity. However, they rarely consider that every notification, every unexpected interaction, and every cognitive process undertaken to understand the product actually consumes the user's finite attention.

In the future, excellent tech products should compete not just on parameters, performance, or AI capabilities, but also on their ability to protect users' attention—a capability long overlooked by both consumers and businesses.

Certainty is the most fundamental and easily overlooked principle for protecting attention. It ensures users always know what will happen next, allows the product to gradually fade from conscious awareness, and returns attention to the truly important people and things.
